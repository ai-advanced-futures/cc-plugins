# StoryBrand SB7 (Miller)

## Purpose

Donald Miller's StoryBrand framework applies the universal structure of story (as used in film, myth, and literature) to marketing messaging. The central thesis is that clarity — not cleverness — is the most powerful marketing asset. Most marketing fails because companies talk about themselves. StoryBrand forces an inversion: the customer is the hero; the brand is the guide. When brands cast themselves as the hero, customers disengage because they cannot see themselves in the narrative. When brands cast the customer as hero and themselves as the wise guide who appears at the right moment, the customer immediately recognizes the story and their place in it. The SB7 framework delivers a complete brand script that drives homepage copy, email sequences, sales decks, and one-liner messaging.

## Core Concepts

**The Core Rule — The Brand Is the Guide, the Customer Is the Hero**
The most common and fatal StoryBrand mistake is a company positioning itself as the hero. "We built the world's best X" is hero language. "You deserve to X — here is how" is guide language. Guides equip heroes. Every messaging audit must check: who has the problem, who has the solution, and who transforms?

**The 7 Parts of the SB7 Framework**

1. **A Character (the Hero)**
   Every story starts with a hero who wants something. In brand messaging, the hero is always the customer. Define the customer's primary desire — not their pain, not their job title, their desire. What does winning look like for them?

2. **Has a Problem**
   The hero encounters conflict that blocks them from getting what they want. StoryBrand identifies three problem levels:
   - **External problem:** The tangible, surface-level obstacle (e.g., "our data is scattered across 12 tools")
   - **Internal problem:** The emotional frustration or self-doubt caused by the external problem (e.g., "I feel like I'm flying blind and my team has lost confidence in me")
   - **Philosophical problem:** The moral injustice — why this problem should not exist in the world (e.g., "Leaders should not have to make million-dollar decisions on gut instinct")
   Great messaging addresses all three levels. Most brands address only the external.

3. **And Meets a Guide**
   The guide (your brand) appears when the hero most needs help. The guide establishes credibility in two ways:
   - **Empathy:** "We understand how hard this is" — the guide has felt or witnessed the hero's pain
   - **Authority:** "We have helped others like you succeed" — demonstrated competence without bragging. Social proof, awards, and statistics belong here — briefly.

4. **Who Gives Them a Plan**
   The plan removes confusion and fear. Humans hesitate to commit when the path is unclear. The plan has two sub-types:
   - **Process plan:** 3-4 simple steps that show how to begin ("Step 1: Schedule a call. Step 2: We audit your stack. Step 3: You get a clear roadmap.")
   - **Agreement plan:** The principles or values you commit to honoring (e.g., "We will never upsell you products you do not need")
   Plans must be simple. Three steps, four at most. Complexity kills commitment.

5. **And Calls Them to Action**
   Stories require a clear call to action. There are two types:
   - **Direct CTA:** "Book a demo" / "Start free trial" — explicit commitment ask
   - **Transitional CTA:** "Download the guide" / "Watch the webinar" — a lower-stakes step for heroes not yet ready to commit
   Both must be present. A website with only a direct CTA loses buyers who need more information. A website with only a transitional CTA never converts.

6. **That Helps Them Avoid Failure**
   Every hero is motivated by the desire to avoid loss as much as the desire to gain. Name what is at stake if the hero does nothing. This is not fear-mongering; it is honest acknowledgment of consequence. What does the hero lose if they fail to act?

7. **And Ends in Success**
   Show the hero what life looks like after they win. Paint a vivid, specific picture of the transformation. The success vision must be aspirational but believable. Vague success statements ("maximize your potential") have zero power. Specific success ("your team ships features 40% faster and nobody misses a sprint deadline") drives action.

**The One-Liner**
A 1-2 sentence compression of the brand script that can be spoken in response to "What do you do?":
"We help [hero] who [problem] by [plan/guide action] so they can [success vision]."

## Application Protocol

**Step 1 — Complete the Brand Script**
Work through all 7 parts in sequence. Each part is a slot to fill with specific content.

**Step 2 — Audit for Hero/Guide Inversion**
Read every line of the brand script. Flag any sentence where the brand is the subject of heroic action. Rewrite to make the customer the subject of transformation.

**Step 3 — Build the One-Liner**
Draft 3 versions using the template. Test each with 3 people outside the company. The winner is the one that causes immediate nodding and the follow-up question "How do you do that?"

**Step 4 — Cascade to GTM Assets**
Map each SB7 element to a website section or sales asset:
- Hero's desire → Homepage headline
- External problem → Above-the-fold subheadline
- Internal problem → Empathy paragraph
- Guide credentials → Social proof section
- Plan → How-it-works section (3 steps)
- Direct CTA → Primary button
- Transitional CTA → Secondary button / lead magnet
- Failure stakes → Brief but honest "cost of inaction" line
- Success vision → Customer story or aspiration section

**Step 5 — Write the Failure and Success Stakes**
These are the most commonly omitted elements. Failure stakes should be 1-2 sentences, grounded in real consequence (revenue lost, competitors winning, team demoralized). Success vision should be a before/after image in 2-3 sentences.
