# Ries & Trout Positioning

## Purpose

Al Ries and Jack Trout established the foundational law of marketing: the battle for customer preference is fought in the mind, not the marketplace. Their central insight is that the human mind can only reliably associate one brand with one concept per category. The strategic imperative is to identify the single word (or tightly bounded concept) you can credibly own, verify that no competitor has already planted that flag, and then drive that word relentlessly across every GTM surface. Diffuse messaging kills positioning. This framework operationalizes the discipline of saying one thing, to one audience, with unrelenting consistency.

## Core Concepts

**The Law of the Mind** — It is better to be first in the mind than first in the marketplace. If a competitor already owns the word you want, you must either reframe the category or choose an adjacent unclaimed word.

**The Owned Word** — The single concept your brand name should trigger in a prospect's mind without any additional context. Examples: Volvo → safety, FedEx → overnight, Slack → channels. The owned word must be:
- Singular (not a list of virtues)
- Category-relevant (answers a real purchase criterion)
- Emotionally or rationally meaningful to the buyer
- Available (not already owned by a dominant competitor)

**Category Reframing** — If the primary word in your category is owned, the alternative is to define a new sub-category where you can be first. Ries & Trout call this "creating a new ladder." Red Bull did not fight Pepsi for "cola" — it created "energy drink."

**The Positioning Line** — A 6-word (or fewer) verbal identity that encodes the owned word. This is a pressure-test tool, not a tagline. If you cannot compress your positioning to 6 words that contain the owned word, the positioning is not yet crisp enough.

**Ladder Position** — In any given category, consumers maintain a mental hierarchy of brands. Position #1 owns the attribute. Position #2 can own the counter-attribute (e.g., if #1 owns "fast," #2 can own "reliable"). Position #3 rarely registers. Know your ladder position and message accordingly.

## Application Protocol

**Step 1 — Name the Word**
Brainstorm 8-12 candidate words that could anchor your positioning. For each candidate ask:
- Is this word a meaningful purchase criterion in the category?
- Does this word authentically represent a genuine product strength?
- Is the word specific enough to differentiate, not so broad it is generic?

Filter to 3 finalist words.

**Step 2 — Verify Availability**
For each finalist word:
1. List the top 5 competitors in your category.
2. Assess whether any competitor is consistently associated with this word in analyst reports, review sites, and customer conversations.
3. Rate availability: Unclaimed / Weakly held / Strongly held / Owned.
4. Disqualify any word rated "Strongly held" or "Owned" unless you plan to reframe the category.

**Step 3 — Select the Word**
Choose the word that is (a) most available and (b) most meaningful to your target. Availability without meaning is irrelevant; meaning without availability is a war you cannot win.

**Step 4 — Validate with the 6-Word Line**
Compress your entire positioning into a 6-word-or-fewer line that contains or strongly implies the chosen word. If you cannot do it cleanly, the word is wrong or the positioning is not yet resolved.

Examples of valid 6-word lines:
- "The database built for real-time." (word: real-time)
- "Security that stops threats before detection." (word: prevention)
- "Forecasting every revenue leader can trust." (word: trust/forecasting)

**Step 5 — Embed and Defend**
The owned word must appear or be strongly implied in: homepage H1, elevator pitch, sales deck title slide, investor narrative, and PR boilerplate. Every quarter, audit whether the word has drifted in external communications. Drift is the most common positioning failure mode.

**Category Reframe Protocol (if primary word is owned)**
1. Identify what the dominant competitor does poorly (their vulnerability).
2. Name a new sub-category that makes their vulnerability structural.
3. Position your product as the obvious leader of that new sub-category.
4. Repeat Steps 1-5 for the new category.
