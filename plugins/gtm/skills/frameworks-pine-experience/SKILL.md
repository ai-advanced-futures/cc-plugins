# Experience Economy (Pine & Gilmore)

## Purpose

B. Joseph Pine II and James H. Gilmore identified that economic value has been progressively extracted from undifferentiated commodities toward staged experiences and, at the frontier, personal transformations. The framework gives GTM strategists a concrete model for diagnosing where their offering sits on the value progression, identifying which stage is achievable, and redesigning the customer journey to deliver and monetize the higher-order offering. Each stage commands materially higher pricing and lower price sensitivity than the one below it. Moving up the progression is the most reliable path to pricing power.

## Core Concepts

**The Five-Stage Economic Progression**

1. **Commodity** — Undifferentiated raw material. Priced by the market. No differentiation possible at the offering level. Customer buys on price alone. Example: raw data feeds, generic cloud storage by the GB.

2. **Good** — Tangible product with standardized features. Modest differentiation through specs. Price premium over commodity but compressed by competition. Example: packaged software, hardware appliances.

3. **Service** — Intangible activity performed on behalf of the customer. Differentiated by reliability, speed, and expertise. Premium over goods. Example: SaaS platforms, managed services, consulting engagements.

4. **Experience** — A memorable event that engages customers personally. The offering is designed to be intrinsically enjoyable, distinctive, and worth retelling. Customers pay for the time they spend, not just the utility. Example: Salesforce Dreamforce, Apple Store Genius Bar, onboarding programs that feel like coaching not setup. Five experience realms: Entertainment, Educational, Escapist, Esthetic.

5. **Transformation** — The highest stage. The customer is changed by the offering. The outcome is a new capability, identity, or life state. The aspirant (who the customer wants to become) is the product. Pricing is set against the value of the transformation, not the inputs. Example: executive coaching, clinical digital health outcomes, career accelerators.

**Experience Staging Principles**
Experiences must be deliberately designed across five dimensions:
- **Theme:** A unified, coherent narrative that orients every touchpoint
- **Impression:** Purposeful cues that reinforce the theme
- **Elimination of negatives:** Remove any sensory or process element inconsistent with the theme
- **Memorability:** Build in narrative peaks and endings (Kahneman's peak-end rule applies)
- **Engagement:** Move customers from passive recipients to active participants

**Premium Pricing at Each Stage**
| Stage | Pricing Basis | Price Sensitivity |
|---|---|---|
| Commodity | Market rate | Extreme |
| Good | Cost + margin | High |
| Service | Value delivered | Moderate |
| Experience | Value of the time | Low |
| Transformation | Value of the outcome | Very low |

## Application Protocol

**Step 1 — Diagnose Current Stage**
Answer three diagnostic questions:
1. What do customers actually pay for — a deliverable, time access, a feeling, or an outcome?
2. Can customers substitute your offering with a generic alternative at meaningfully lower cost?
3. Do customers describe their experience with you in emotional terms, not just functional terms?

Place the current offering on the progression.

**Step 2 — Identify Target Stage**
Determine the highest achievable stage given your category, buyer psychology, and competitive set. Not every offering can or should be a Transformation. B2B software typically targets Service → Experience. High-stakes, outcome-dependent engagements (health, career, financial) can reach Transformation.

**Step 3 — Map the Customer Journey as a Stage**
Redesign the customer journey (from first touchpoint through deep adoption) as a deliberately staged experience:
- **Set the stage:** Define the experience theme in one sentence
- **Act 1 — Arrival:** What is the first impression? Does it signal a different category?
- **Act 2 — Engagement:** What are the peak moments of value delivery?
- **Act 3 — Climax:** What is the memorable "wow" moment that makes this worth retelling?
- **Act 4 — Resolution:** How does the experience conclude? Does the customer leave changed?

**Step 4 — Eliminate Experience Negatives**
Audit every customer-facing touchpoint for elements that undermine the experience theme. Common negatives: generic onboarding emails, support tickets that feel transactional, dashboards that look like every competitor's, pricing pages that feel like a trap.

**Step 5 — Design the Transformation Outcome (if applicable)**
If targeting Transformation:
- Define the "aspirant" — who does the customer want to become through this product?
- Build before/after evidence into the product (progress tracking, capability milestones)
- Price against the value of becoming the aspirant, not the cost of the service
- Use transformation language in all messaging ("become," "achieve," "lead," not "use," "access," "manage")

**Step 6 — Price for the Stage**
Adjust pricing model to match the value stage:
- Experience stage: introduce event-based pricing, tiered access, or annual membership models
- Transformation stage: outcomes-based pricing, milestone-gated payment, long-term contracts with success metrics
